\begin{abstract}
Legal documents are fundamental to civic participation, yet their inherent complexity creates significant barriers to comprehension, particularly for individuals with cognitive and learning disabilities. This study addresses the challenge of legal text inaccessibility by proposing LASCAS (Legal Accessibility and Simplification for Cognitive Aid System), a local-first prototype designed to simplify Brazilian legal Portuguese. The work is grounded in cognitive accessibility principles, the Plain Language movement, and Automatic Text Simplification (ATS), combining modern NLP techniques with semantic-integrity safeguards and accessibility-oriented presentation. Empirically, a formative pre-test with 3 legal-domain participants supported the adoption of the \texttt{medium} simplification level, while a broader questionnaire study with 30 participants produced consistently positive results: Perceived Ease of Use and Learnability both reached a mean of 4.68/5, Perceived Usefulness reached 4.63/5, and the SUS-style composite averaged 90.4/100, with 86.7\% of participants rating the experience at 80 or above. In particular, the high Ease of Use/Learnability scores and the 90.4/100 usability composite suggest that the prototype is already effective as a support tool for the initial reading, comprehension, and triage of legal documents. Overall, the results indicate that LASCAS is a promising approach to making legal information more approachable and to advancing access to justice and digital inclusion.
\palavraschave{Text Simplification. Cognitive Accessibility. Natural Language Processing. Legal Tech. Transformer Models.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent advances in large language models and Natural Language Processing (NLP) are creating increasingly sophisticated systems capable of processing complex legal language and supporting a growing range of legal tasks \cite{he2026legal}. This technological advancement holds particular promise for the legal domain, where documents ranging from judicial rulings to terms of service form the bedrock of civic life. However, a profound disconnect exists between the importance of these texts and their accessibility. The specialized language of law, or "Legalese," is characterized by an intricate vocabulary and convoluted sentence structures, creating a formidable barrier to comprehension for non-experts \cite{luger2024towards}.

The scale of this challenge is amplified by literacy statistics. In Brazil, data indicates that a significant portion of the population lacks the functional literacy skills required to parse complex, high-stakes documents \cite{alves2023automatic}. This gap is not merely academic; it has profound consequences. The inability to understand a contract or a court summary impedes an individual's ability to make informed decisions and exercise their rights, creating a dependency on legal professionals whose services can be prohibitively expensive. This exacerbates social inequities and limits access to justice, a problem that automated simplification systems are uniquely positioned to address \cite{agrawal2024do}.

This problem of inaccessible legal language is a recognized societal challenge, prompting action from governmental bodies worldwide. Initiatives such as the Plain Writing Act of 2010 in the United States and resolutions from Brazil's National Council of Justice (CNJ) explicitly call for clearer communication \cite{alves2023automatic}. The development of automated systems for legal text simplification is, therefore, a direct response to a pressing need to bridge the communication gap between the judiciary and the populace. Such technology aligns with a global movement towards making legal information a public good, accessible to all regardless of educational background.

The response to this challenge is increasingly found at the intersection of law and artificial intelligence, often termed "Legal Tech" \cite{ribeiro2024iajuridica}. Specifically, the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP) offers the tools to analyze and, more importantly, transform complex legal language at scale \cite{ariai2024nlp}. This technological intervention is not merely for convenience; it is a necessary step to operationalize the principles of cognitive accessibility and Plain Language for the vast and growing body of digital legal information. This work is situated within this exact context, proposing a computational approach to mitigate a deeply rooted communication barrier.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

The core problem addressed by this thesis is that legal texts in Brazilian Portuguese are characterized by a high degree of linguistic and structural complexity, rendering them functionally inaccessible to a significant portion of the population \cite{mello2023acessojustica}. This complexity is not a mere stylistic inconvenience; it functions as a systemic barrier that perpetuates a dependency on legal intermediaries, transforming access to justice from a public right into a service contingent on financial resources or specialized education \cite{silva2023barreiras}. Recent scholarship on legislative drafting similarly argues that laws intended for broad public audiences must be written with understandability and accessibility in view, rather than primarily for specialist readers \cite{zariski2026plain}.

This inaccessibility has a disproportionate impact on individuals with cognitive or learning disabilities \cite{barbosa2024inclusao}. For this group, the high cognitive load imposed by convoluted syntax and low-frequency jargon directly contravenes foundational principles of inclusive design and web accessibility \cite{chen2022cognitive}. Consequently, this inaccessibility undermines the principles of informed consent, equal access to justice \cite{santos2023desafioslegais}, and digital inclusion \cite{agrawal2024do}. This last point is critical in the current technological landscape: as essential civic and commercial interactions move online, this linguistic barrier creates a "second-level digital divide," where citizens may have \textit{access} to digital platforms (e.g., terms of service, government portals) but are denied \textit{comprehension} of their rights and obligations \cite{fernandes2024digitaldivide}.

\subsection{Research Question and Objectives}

This study is guided by a central research question and a set of objectives designed to systematically explore the problem space and potential solutions.

Research Question:
\textit{How can modern Natural Language Processing techniques, particularly those based on the Transformer architecture, be effectively applied to simplify Brazilian Portuguese legal texts to improve their cognitive accessibility in accordance with Plain Language principles?}

Objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To establish the conceptual and technical foundations of LASCAS by relating cognitive accessibility, Plain Language, and Automatic Text Simplification in the context of Brazilian Portuguese legal documents.
    \item To develop the LASCAS prototype as a local-first architecture capable of simplifying real Brazilian legal documents (HTML and PDF) while preserving essential legal information.
    \item To evaluate the prototype in terms of perceived understanding, usability, and its main strengths and limitations for improving access to legal information.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

To situate the LASCAS project, this section outlines the foundational concepts at the intersection of accessibility, linguistics, and computation. We first explore the "why"—the principles of cognitive accessibility and Plain Language that define the goal. We then detail the "how"—the core technologies of Automatic Text Simplification (ATS) and the Transformer models that enable it. The specific features that define the complexity of legal text, which this project directly targets, are well-documented and can be categorized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textit{Lexical Complexity:} The pervasive use of domain-specific jargon, archaic terminology, and foreign phrases unfamiliar to non-experts.
 \item \textit{Syntactic Complexity:} The prevalence of long, convoluted sentences containing multiple nested clauses and the passive voice, which can obscure meaning and increase processing difficulty.
 \item \textit{High Information Density:} The condensation of numerous conditions and exceptions into single, dense paragraphs, demanding high levels of sustained attention and working memory.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cognitive Accessibility and Plain Language}

Cognitive accessibility is an aspect of inclusive design focused on making information understandable for people with a wide range of cognitive and learning disabilities affecting memory, attention, and language comprehension \cite{w3c2023wcag}. The W3C's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) provide a framework for this, with Guideline 3.1, "Readable," being particularly pertinent. Its intent is to ensure users can correctly interpret text. Key success criteria under this guideline that directly motivate this thesis include \cite{w3c2023wcag}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Unusual Words (3.1.3):} Recommends providing a mechanism for identifying definitions of words or phrases used in an unusual or restricted way, including jargon and idioms.
    \item \textit{Abbreviations (3.1.4):} Calls for a mechanism to be available for identifying the expanded form or meaning of abbreviations.
    \item \textit{Reading Level (3.1.5):} Considered a cornerstone of this work, this criterion recommends that when content requires an advanced reading ability, a supplementary version that does not require such ability should be provided.
\end{itemize}

Collectively, these criteria establish a direct mandate for text simplification as a necessary accommodation. While cognitive accessibility defines the goal, the Plain Language framework provides the methodology. Plain Language is communication so clear that the intended audience can easily find what they need, understand it, and use it effectively. Its principles offer concrete guidelines for rewriting complex text, which can be operationalized by an automated system \cite{iso2023plain}. Key principles include structural simplicity (short sentences, clear headings), lexical simplicity (common words instead of jargon), and directness (using the active voice). Manually applying this methodology to the vast corpus of legal documents is impractical \cite{alves2023automatic}. This is where NLP serves as the enabling tool, providing the means to automate the Plain Language methodology at scale. This thesis is therefore positioned at the intersection of these fields, aiming to operationalize a design philosophy (Plain Language) with a specific technology (NLP) to achieve a critical goal (Cognitive Accessibility).

\subsection{Automatic Text Simplification with Transformers}

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a subfield of AI concerned with enabling computers to understand and manipulate human language. Within NLP, Automatic Text Simplification (ATS) aims to transform a text into an easier-to-read version while preserving its original meaning \cite{alves2023automatic}. It is distinct from summarization, which inherently reduces information content. ATS operates through two primary sub-tasks: lexical and syntactic simplification.

The majority of state-of-the-art NLP models are based on the Transformer architecture \cite{alissa2023text}. The Transformer's key innovation was its reliance on attention mechanisms, which proved highly effective at capturing long-range dependencies between words. It follows an encoder-decoder structure well-suited for text-to-text tasks like simplification. The encoder processes the input complex sentence to build a context-aware numerical representation, and the decoder uses this representation to generate the simplified output one token at a time. The core of this architecture is the multi-head self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to weigh the importance of different words in the input when processing the sequence. This enables it to jointly attend to information from different representational subspaces, enhancing its ability to capture the complex relationships between words in a sentence \cite{alissa2023text}. This architecture provides the powerful foundation upon which modern simplification models are built.

\section{Related Work}

Related work was identified through a reproducible, funnel-style search across Google Scholar, ACL Anthology, Scopus, and DBLP. A broad search combining the terms ``legal text simplification,'' ``plain language,'' and ``law/legal,'' with a 2022--current year filter, yielded \emph{$\sim 60$} items screened by title and abstract. Scope was then narrowed to \textit{Automatic Text Simplification (ATS)} in the legal domain through combinations of ``text simplification,'' ``legal,'' and document types such as contracts, judgments, and statutes, alongside model terms such as Transformer, T5, and BART, reducing the pool to \emph{$\sim 25$}. Locale-sensitive searches also targeted Portuguese and Russian efforts. After removing duplicates and near-duplicates, \emph{$\sim 14$} studies remained. Inclusion then required either empirical ATS on legal data with metrics and/or human judgments, or peer-reviewed conceptual frameworks grounding plain-language and AI-assisted rephrasing for legal accessibility. After full-text screening, \emph{6} primary papers remained. \textbf{\cite{garimella2022insights}} explored text simplification (TS) for legal contracts, highlighting the lack of parallel data as a key challenge. By evaluating unsupervised methods and a supervised BART model, they found significant \textit{disagreement among legal experts on simplicity} and \textit{weak correlations between automatic metrics and human judgments} \cite{garimella2022insights}. Their work underscored the trade-offs between fluency, meaning preservation, and simplification level.

\textbf{\cite{alves2023automatic}} focus on Brazilian Portuguese \textit{judicial summaries}, tackling data scarcity by generating a synthetic parallel dataset with the unsupervised MUSS model \cite{alves2023automatic}. This data was then used to train supervised Transformer and NMT models. Their evaluation showed that most methods, especially the Transformer model and MUSS combined with translation, \textit{improved Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) scores} over the original summaries.

\textbf{\cite{athugodage2024transfer}} address data scarcity for Russian legal text by creating a parallel corpus that pairs official legal documents with plain-language explanations from the "Rossiyskaya Gazeta" newspaper \cite{athugodage2024transfer}. Using this dataset, they \textit{fine-tuned T5 and GPT models} which, despite poor ROUGE scores, achieved good results in SARI and readability metrics and often exceeded human-written commentaries in human evaluation.

\textbf{\cite{toivonen2025jelt}} discuss plain-language communication and the role of AI within the emerging paradigm of “personalized law,” arguing that user-centered legal design and clear wording are prerequisites for accessibility rather than optional stylistic choices \cite{toivonen2025jelt}. They caution that AI should augment—not replace—human judgment, recommending standards and oversight to avoid opacity and loss of legal nuance. Although not an empirical simplification benchmark, the paper positions plain language and responsible AI as foundations for scalable legal accessibility.

\textbf{\cite{zimina2025gai}} examine generative AI models as content-rephrasing assistants for producing Plain Language and Easy-to-Read versions of legal texts in multilingual settings \cite{zimina2025gai}. Their case-based analysis shows that, although GAI can substantially simplify phrasing and structure, it still lacks functional legal awareness; consequently, effective workflows require human oversight, clear domain guidelines, and review tools to prevent semantic and pragmatic losses.

Overall, the literature evolves from exposing the limits of existing legal TS methods to proposing data-creation strategies and, more recently, broader plain-language and human-in-the-loop perspectives. Across these studies, the shared challenge remains improving readability without compromising legal meaning, especially in data-scarce settings. This gap directly motivates the LASCAS proposal.

\section{LASCAS Model}

To address the problem of legal text inaccessibility, this thesis proposes the conceptualization and development of the LASCAS (Legal Accessibility and Simplification for Cognitive Aid System) prototype. LASCAS is envisioned as an Automatic Text Simplification (ATS) system that leverages modern Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques to transform complex legal Brazilian Portuguese into a more understandable form aligned with Plain Language principles. The primary goal of LASCAS is to enhance the cognitive accessibility of legal documents by systematically reducing their lexical and syntactic complexity, thereby lowering the cognitive load required for comprehension.

\subsection{Architecture}

LASCAS is a local-first pipeline that ingests HTML or PDF, normalizes legal artifacts, applies \emph{level-aware token safety}, and then simplifies the text using a hybrid core that combines a local LLM (via Ollama) with deterministic fallbacks. A thin browser extension invokes the local API and renders results side-by-side. The main components are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Local API (Node/Express):} the primary JSON endpoint \texttt{/simplify} orchestrates the end-to-end flow for HTML and PDF (via URL or Base64). In addition, the prototype exposes \texttt{/simplify-pdf} for binary PDF uploads (\texttt{application/pdf}), which reduces payload overhead for large files and improves robustness in real usage.
\item \textit{PDF Extraction:} robust text extraction using \texttt{pdfjs-dist} legacy build; includes a geometry-based reconstruction that re-assembles lines by $x$–$y$ coordinates and adaptive gap heuristics, plus page-gluing rules that avoid spurious blank paragraphs.
\item \textit{Preprocessing:} normalization of legal artifacts (numbers, punctuation, diacritics, currency, dates), sentence splitting with abbreviation shielding, and paragraph-aware chunking for the condensation levels. Token safety is level-aware: in \texttt{light}, placeholders are used to protect critical items before any LLM call. In \texttt{medium}/\texttt{strong}, the system prioritizes the \emph{preservation and validation} of essential items (e.g., dates, values, percentages, legal citations, registry/process identifiers, and negations), while PII/qualification fields may be omitted by design.
\item \textit{Simplification Core:} hybrid engine that (i) prompts the local model \texttt{llama3.1:8b-instruct} via Ollama (configured in the prototype through \texttt{LASCAS\_MODEL}); (ii) rejects unusable outputs (timeouts, meta-preambles, or overly similar rewrites); (iii) deterministic restructuring fallback that enforces visible structure (short lines, headings, bullets); and (iv) glossary first-occurrence annotations for unusual words and abbreviations (WCAG 3.1 intent).
\item \textit{Integrity Layer (level-aware):} semantic safety check that validates preservation of \emph{essential} legal information. In \texttt{light}, integrity is strict (high fidelity, near-complete preservation). In \texttt{medium}/\texttt{strong}, integrity is configured for summarization: it still preserves dates, values, percentages, legal citations and registry/process references, but allows non-essential personal qualifications (e.g., CPF/RG, address, repetitions) to be dropped to achieve meaningful condensation.
\item \textit{Extension Shell (MV3):} popup selects simplification level and messages the content script; CSS sidebar is injected as a web-accessible resource for side-by-side viewing. PDFs can be forwarded as Base64 through \texttt{/simplify} or as binary uploads through \texttt{/simplify-pdf}, depending on browser constraints and file size.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{light} level focuses on sentence-level rewriting (high fidelity, near-original length), while \texttt{medium} and \texttt{strong} apply condensation with a guaranteed-shrink heuristic fallback. \subsection{Aspects of Implementation}

This section details key technical choices and components within the LASCAS prototype, focusing on robustness, legal-specific processing, safety mechanisms, and integration points.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{PDF robustness:}
The extractor uses \texttt{pdfjs-dist} legacy build with explicit worker/CMAP paths and applies a geometry-based reconstruction that re-assembles lines by $y$‐banding and adaptive gap thresholds (percentiles of glyph gaps per line). It also fixes \emph{dash-line breaks} in numbers (\texttt{1-\textbackslash n2} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{1-2}), collapses excessive newlines, and glues page breaks using sentence-aware heuristics to avoid accidental paragraph splits.

\item \textit{Legal-aware normalization and token safety (level-aware):}
The normalizer repairs Brazilian formats (\texttt{R\$ 133.569,32}), digit spacing artifacts (\texttt{133.569, 32} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{133.569,32}), and OCR-style issues typical of notary deeds. Token safety is level-aware:
(i) in \texttt{light}, placeholders are used to protect critical content before LLM calls (numbers, dates, values, legal citations, negations, and fragile legal terms) and are restored after rewriting;
(ii) in \texttt{medium}/\texttt{strong}, the system focuses on preserving and validating \emph{essential} items (dates, values, percentages, legal citations, and registry/process identifiers), while allowing non-essential qualification/PII fields to be omitted to achieve meaningful condensation.

\item \textit{Sentence splitting and chunking:}
Abbreviations (e.g., \emph{Art., Dr., Av.}) are shielded to avoid spurious splits. In \texttt{light}, rewriting is performed sentence-by-sentence within paragraphs. In \texttt{medium}/\texttt{strong}, the input is split into paragraph-aware chunks (520--1050 words) to respect token budgets and latency caps.

\item \textit{LLM adapter and latency control:}
The adapter targets a local Ollama endpoint and prompts \texttt{llama3.1:8b-instruct} (set through \texttt{LASCAS\_MODEL} in the prototype setup). It computes a dynamic \texttt{num\_predict} from input size and the per-level target, enforces per-call budgets, and uses soft timeouts (via abort signals). Cold starts receive a one-time boost for the first calls. Outputs that are empty, contain meta-preambles, or fail acceptance checks (e.g., insufficient change for rewrite; insufficient shrink for condensation) are rejected.

\item \textit{Deterministic structuring:}
The fallback enforces visible structure: short lines, stable headings, bulletization of semicolon-delimited lists, and targeted legal phrasing fixes. This guarantees readability even without the LLM and reduces the risk of returning nearly unchanged legalese.

\item \textit{Glossary and cognitive accessibility:}
On the first occurrence, selected legal terms are annotated inline with simple definitions (Portuguese), which supports WCAG 3.1 intent for unusual words and abbreviations.

\item \textit{Semantic integrity gate (level-aware):}
Before returning, the output is validated to preserve essential legal information. In \texttt{light}, integrity is strict and aims to preserve nearly all numeric and legal references. In \texttt{medium}/\texttt{strong}, integrity is configured for summarization: it still preserves dates, values, percentages, legal citations and registry/process references, but allows omission of non-essential personal qualifications (e.g., CPF/RG/address) when they do not affect the legal meaning of the excerpt.

\item \textit{Extension integration:}
The MV3 manifest injects a content script and exposes \texttt{sidebar.css} as a web-accessible resource. The popup selects \texttt{level} and communicates via tab messaging. For PDFs, the prototype supports (a) forwarding Base64 through \texttt{/simplify} and (b) forwarding raw bytes through \texttt{/simplify-pdf}. For local \texttt{file://} PDFs, the user may need to explicitly allow file access for the extension in the browser settings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

The current prototype should be understood as a local-first proof of concept, and some of its limitations stem directly from that design choice. Because LASCAS performs extraction, preprocessing, validation, and simplification locally, execution quality is influenced by the computational resources available on the user’s machine. This becomes more noticeable with long legal documents and larger PDF files, which may require more memory, longer inference time, and more aggressive chunking. While the system includes warm-up routines, timeout controls, acceptance checks, and deterministic fallbacks to preserve responsiveness, local execution on commodity hardware still imposes practical limits on throughput and latency.

Another relevant limitation concerns the language model itself. Although the prototype is configured to simplify Brazilian legal Portuguese, the underlying model is not a model specifically trained or fine-tuned on a broad, curated corpus of Brazilian legal simplification pairs. In practice, this means that part of the system’s effectiveness comes from prompt engineering, preprocessing, integrity validation, and fallback logic rather than from domain-specialized learning alone. As a consequence, the output may still miss some stylistic, terminological, or contextual nuances that are typical of Brazilian legal and notarial documents, especially in highly formal or institution-specific texts.

The prototype also depends on a glossary layer to better support cognitive accessibility. This is consistent with the goal of the project, since legal comprehension is not improved only by shortening or rephrasing sentences; unusual terms, abbreviations, and domain-specific expressions often also need explanation. At the same time, this introduces a limitation, because the glossary is necessarily finite and manually curated. When the input contains rare terminology, regional usages, institution-specific abbreviations, or very specialized legal expressions that are not yet covered, the simplified text may remain partially difficult even when the syntactic structure has been improved.

There are also limitations in PDF processing. The current extraction pipeline works best when the PDF contains an actual text layer, that is, when the text can be selected normally by the user. In contrast, scanned PDFs or image-only PDFs, in which the page visually contains text but no selectable textual content is embedded, are not handled with the same reliability because the prototype does not currently include a dedicated OCR stage. For this reason, document quality at the input level has a direct impact on the quality of the extracted text and, consequently, on the quality of the simplification.

A further limitation appears in documents with repeated content across pages, such as headers, footer lines, registry references, or recurring institutional metadata. The prototype includes heuristics to reduce the impact of these repetitions, but this remains a difficult case because repeated page elements may sometimes be irrelevant noise and sometimes contain legally relevant information. In practice, this means that some repeated text may still be preserved when it should ideally be removed, while in other cases the system must act conservatively to avoid discarding information that could matter to the legal meaning of the document.

Given these constraints, the implementation includes a small number of targeted heuristics refined during development and testing. This was a practical decision to improve robustness in a domain where legal documents frequently present recurring formatting artifacts, repeated institutional structures, and document-specific conventions that are not always handled well by general-purpose pipelines. In the context of a prototype, these adjustments helped ensure more stable behavior across the tested files while preserving the central objective of the work: simplifying legal text while maintaining essential legal information. Future versions of LASCAS should gradually reduce the need for such case-specific rules through broader datasets, stronger document parsing, OCR support, and models better adapted to Brazilian legal Portuguese.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}

We adopt a mixed attitudinal approach that combines perceived usability (via the System Usability Scale, SUS) with acceptance constructs (via the Technology Acceptance Model, TAM). This choice is supported by recent SUS benchmarking work in applied settings and systematic evidence of TAM’s suitability for technology‐adoption research \cite{hyzy2022susbench, rosli2022tamreview}. Our design also follows studies that explicitly integrate SUS and TAM when evaluating interactive systems \cite{chuenyindee2022integrating}.

\subsection{Participants and Procedure}
Participants use the LASCAS browser extension to simplify at least one real legal document (HTML or PDF) and then complete the questionnaire. The procedure is unmoderated and consists of three steps: brief task instructions, task execution with the extension, and a post‐task questionnaire (Likert items plus one open‐ended question).

\subsection{Instrument: Likert Scale and Items}
All items use a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = \textit{Strongly disagree}, 2 = \textit{Disagree}, 3 = \textit{Neither}, 4 = \textit{Agree}, 5 = \textit{Strongly agree}). The ten statements are \textbf{randomized} to avoid presenting the positive/negative pair of the same construct consecutively, an arrangement recommended to reduce response artifacts in lightweight usability evaluations \cite{robertson2020discount}. The complete list of items is shown in Table~\ref{tab:survey-items}. The statements cover five constructs: Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU), Learnability, Perceived Usefulness (PU), Performance (latency/throughput), and Behavioral Intention (BI).

\subsection{Scoring and Data Handling}

This subsection outlines the scoring pipeline used to derive construct-level indices and an optional SUS-style composite. We first define the coding scheme, then compute construct means, perform an inconsistency check, assess reliability, and specify reporting outputs.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Coding:} Responses are mapped to integers 1–5 in ascending agreement. Negative items (Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9, Q10) are reverse‐coded via $\mathrm{rev}(x)=6-x$. This scoring is consistent with contemporary SUS/TAM practice in mixed instruments \cite{robertson2020discount}.

\item \textit{Construct scores:} We compute per‐construct means using the positive/negative pairs in their randomized positions:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{PEOU}&=\mathrm{mean}(Q1,\,\mathrm{rev}(Q8)),\quad
\mathrm{Learnability}=\mathrm{mean}(Q2,\,\mathrm{rev}(Q6)),\\
\mathrm{PU}&=\mathrm{mean}(Q5,\,\mathrm{rev}(Q9)),\quad
\mathrm{Performance}=\mathrm{mean}(Q3,\,\mathrm{rev}(Q7)),\\
\mathrm{BI}&=\mathrm{mean}(Q4,\,\mathrm{rev}(Q10)).
\end{aligned}
\]
For an optional SUS‐style composite (0–100), we linearly rescale the summed item scores, following benchmarking practice in recent literature \cite{hyzy2022susbench}.

\item \textit{Inconsistency check:} For each pair, we compute $\Delta=\lvert Q^{+}-\mathrm{rev}(Q^{-})\rvert$; large discrepancies (e.g., $\Delta>2$) are flagged as potential inattentive responses. Flags are reported as a data‐quality indicator and not used for automatic exclusion.

\item \textit{Reliability and analysis:} We report internal consistency (Cronbach’s $\alpha$) per construct and for the full scale, along with descriptive statistics (mean $\pm$ SD). When comparing system variants, we employ nonparametric tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed‐rank) or mixed models, with adjustments for multiple comparisons as needed. The TAM lens supports interpretation of PEOU/PU as antecedents of BI in our analysis narrative.

\item \textit{Outputs and Reporting:} We present: (i) construct means for PEOU, Learnability, PU, Performance, and BI; (ii) the optional SUS‐style composite (0–100); (iii) the proportion of responses flagged by the inconsistency check; and (iv) key themes from the open‐ended answers. This structure aligns with recent integrated SUS+TAM evaluations in educational and productivity technologies.
\end{itemize}



\section{Results and Discussion}

This section presents the empirical results obtained with LASCAS and discusses them in light of the goals established in Sections~4 and~5. Rather than treating evaluation as a purely benchmark-driven exercise, this work adopted an applied and iterative validation strategy centered on real legal documents, human perception, and cognitive accessibility. This framing is coherent with the practical character of the prototype and with the data-scarcity problem already identified in related work on legal simplification \cite{garimella2022insights, alves2023automatic, athugodage2024transfer}.

\subsection{Evaluation Scope and Rationale}

The empirical stage was organized in two complementary moments. First, a formative pre-test was conducted with three participants from the legal domain using three public legal files that were anonymized for the study. This smaller round had an exploratory role: it helped verify whether the prototype had reached a satisfactory maturity threshold for a broader trial, identify which failure modes still required attention, and define a feasible evaluation format. A key outcome of this pre-test was the decision to adopt the \texttt{medium} simplification level in the larger study, since it offered the best balance between readability gain, semantic preservation, and task duration.

Second, a broader attitudinal evaluation was conducted with the questionnaire described in Section~5. In this stage, each participant assessed one legal document simplified at the \texttt{medium} level. This decision intentionally reduced reading burden and kept the session short enough to avoid fatigue, while still allowing participants to compare the original and simplified versions with adequate attention. \subsection{Discussion and Positioning in the Literature}

The results position LASCAS in an interesting place relative to the studies reviewed in Section~3. Prior work has consistently highlighted three central tensions in legal simplification: the scarcity of parallel data, the difficulty of evaluating simplification quality, and the risk of semantic loss. LASCAS does not resolve these tensions once and for all, but the empirical evidence suggests that its architecture offers a productive way of navigating them in practice.

A first contribution is operational. While several related studies focus primarily on training strategies or corpus construction, LASCAS contributes a full prototype perspective. The system is not only a simplification model; it is a document-oriented workflow that includes extraction, legal-aware normalization, level-aware simplification, integrity validation, glossary support, and browser-side presentation. For accessibility purposes, this matters because users experience the whole interaction chain, not only the local quality of sentence rewriting.

A second contribution concerns evaluation. As already suggested by Garimella et al.~(2022), automatic metrics and human judgment do not necessarily move in parallel in legal simplification. The present study extends that point by showing that user-centered evidence can be organized around perceived clarity, usefulness, and workflow fit, rather than around simplification scores alone. In this sense, the contribution of LASCAS is not to replace metric-oriented research, but to complement it with evidence of value in realistic use.

A third contribution lies in how the prototype is positioned. Participants repeatedly framed the system as support for initial reading, triage, and communication, not as a substitute for specialized legal interpretation. This is a productive framing for the current stage of legal AI: tools of this kind add most value when they mediate access, not when they claim autonomous legal authority.

\section{Conclusion}

This thesis investigated how contemporary Natural Language Processing techniques can be employed to simplify Brazilian Portuguese legal texts with cognitive accessibility as a guiding objective. In response to this problem, the work proposed and developed LASCAS, a \emph{local-first} prototype capable of receiving legal documents in HTML or PDF, processing them, and presenting simplified versions through a browser-integrated interface.

The main contribution of the thesis can be understood at three levels. At the conceptual level, it brings together cognitive accessibility, plain language, and automatic text simplification in a domain where insufficient comprehension can affect autonomy, informed decision-making, and access to legal information. At the technical level, it shows that legal simplification in Brazilian Portuguese benefits from a hybrid architecture in which the language model operates alongside preprocessing, protection of essential information, validation, and presentation layers. At the empirical level, it shows that this combination already yields a prototype perceived by users as useful, easy to operate, and relevant for real tasks involving initial reading and document triage.

The results suggest that the value of LASCAS does not lie only in rewriting difficult passages, but in reorganizing access to the legal document. Rather than competing with specialized legal reading, the tool acts as an intermediate layer of approach, allowing users to grasp the central theme, the function of the document, and the points that deserve closer attention more quickly. This is particularly relevant because it shifts automatic simplification from a merely experimental role toward a practical support function.

Another important contribution lies in the methodological stance of the work. By relying on real documents, user evaluation with distinct participant profiles, and a solution executable in a local environment, the thesis brings the problem closer to realistic conditions of use. This makes the results less dependent on idealized scenarios and more connected to the kinds of constraints that typically accompany applied systems in legal and institutional settings. It is also worth noting that the present results were obtained with a lightweight local model rather than high-end inference infrastructure, which means future iterations may benefit not only from broader data and refinement, but also from stronger hardware or more efficient models that can reduce latency pressure and support more stable simplification across longer documents.

From the perspective of the research field, the thesis reinforces an idea that appears recurrently in the literature but is not always explored in depth: simplifying legal texts is not only a text-generation challenge, but also a mediation problem between specialized language and public understanding. For this reason, the quality of a solution in this area depends not only on model behavior, but also on how the tool structures reading, preserves essential content, and fits into an understandable workflow.

Looking ahead, LASCAS opens several meaningful directions for future work. These include broader evaluations with larger samples and objective comprehension tasks, expansion of document coverage, richer context-aware glossary support, more robust handling of complex PDFs, and integration with complementary reading aids. There is also a clear opportunity to explore how systems of this kind can be incorporated into institutional communication, legal assistance, or first-contact guidance workflows.

In summary, LASCAS demonstrates that AI-assisted legal simplification can generate concrete gains in perceived clarity, usefulness, and accessibility without depending on excessively complex infrastructure. For the scope of this thesis, that already constitutes a relevant result. More than presenting an isolated experiment, the work offers a solid basis for future evolution and supports the idea that language technologies can contribute in a practical way to reducing the distance between legal documents and the people who need to understand them.
